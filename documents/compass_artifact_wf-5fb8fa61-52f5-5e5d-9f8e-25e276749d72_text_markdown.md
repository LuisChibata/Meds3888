# Clinical Fit in Patient–Therapist Matching: What the Research Shows and How to Use It

The best evidence on clinical fit says this: match patients to therapists on the therapist's **measured** track record with the patient's main problems, and match the **level of care** to how severe and complex the case is. Do not match on the specialties therapists list for themselves, because the research shows therapists cannot reliably judge their own problem-specific strengths.\[1\] The strongest single study, Constantino et al. (2021, *JAMA Psychiatry*), found that outcome-based matching beat usual assignment with moderate-to-large effects (d = 0.50–0.75).\[2\] That study had adults, one US health system and therapists with years of outcome data, so it does not transfer directly to young people in Sydney.

## TL;DR
- **Strongest clinical-fit criteria:** (1) the therapist's measured outcomes with the patient's top problem domains, and avoiding therapists with poor measured outcomes in any of the patient's problem areas; (2) severity and complexity, which decide the level of care (low vs high intensity) and make matching matter more; (3) an evidence-based modality for the presenting problem (e.g., CBT-type therapy for youth anxiety).
- **Therapists matter, but modestly on average:** Johns et al. (2019, *Clinical Psychology Review*) found that "approximately 5% of the variance in outcomes was attributable to the therapist," which sits "within the 3–7% indicated by the previous systematic review (Baldwin & Imel 2013)." That share is larger in routine care and for more severe patients. Whether therapists are good at some problems and weak at others is still debated: Kraus et al. (2011, 2016) say yes, Nissen-Lie et al. (2016) say effectiveness is mostly global.
- **For your Sydney 16–24 system:** use Australia's IAR-DST (8 domains → 5 levels of care) and validated measures (K10, PHQ-9, GAD-7, risk screening, functioning) for triage. Use credentials and modality as a "cold-start" stand-in, and build in routine outcome tracking so real therapist-by-problem track records can build up over time. No published trial has tested outcome-based therapist matching in young people or in Australia.

## Key Findings

### 1. What "clinical fit" means here (and what it is not)
- **Clinical fit** = how well the therapist's proven ability and the treatment type suit the patient's clinical needs: presenting problem(s), symptom profile, severity, risk and complexity.
- **Not clinical fit** (but still useful): **preference matching** (giving patients the type of therapy or therapist they want), **demographic/cultural matching** (e.g., gender, ethnicity, language) and **therapeutic alliance** (the quality of the working relationship, which only develops after therapy starts). Preference accommodation mainly lowers dropout. Swift et al. (2018) found dropout odds ratio 1.79 and a small outcome effect, d = 0.28.\[3\] So preference is a strong *second layer* on top of clinical fit, not a replacement for it.
- **Assumption flagged:** I treat "track record" as *measured patient outcomes* (routine outcome monitoring), not years of experience or listed specialties. That is how the main matching studies define it.

### 2. The core matching trial: Constantino et al. (2021) — verified
**Citation:** Constantino MJ, Boswell JF, Coyne AE, Swales TP, Kraus DR. Effect of Matching Therapists to Patients vs Assignment as Usual on Adult Psychotherapy Outcomes: A Randomized Clinical Trial. *JAMA Psychiatry*. 2021;78(9):960–969. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.1221.\[4\] PMID 34106240; PMCID PMC8190692;\[5\] ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02990000.\[6\]

**Design:**
- Double-blind RCT at 6 community mental health clinics in Cleveland, Ohio, run between November 2017 and April 2019. Therapists treated both matched and usual-care patients and did not know which group a patient was in. Therapy itself was not changed.\[6\]
- Before the trial, each therapist received a "report card" across the 12 domains of the **Treatment Outcome Package (TOP)**, built from 15 or more past cases (mean 28.5 patients each). The 12 domains are depression, quality of life, mania, panic/somatic anxiety, psychosis, substance misuse, social conflict, sexual functioning, sleep, suicidality, violence and work functioning.\[6\]
- Outcomes were **risk-adjusted** against a reference sample of about 28,000 patients. In each domain, a therapist was classed as *effective* (patients reliably beat their expected change), *neutral* or *ineffective* (patients reliably fell short).\[6\]
- Matched patients got a shortlist ranked over 5 levels. The top level was a therapist effective on the patient's 3 most elevated domains and not ineffective on any domain. The bottom level was a therapist not effective on any elevated domain but not ineffective anywhere. Staff then chose from the shortlist based on practical limits such as insurance.\[6\]

**Sample:**
- 1,329 people were screened and 288 randomised. 218 patients treated by 48 therapists were analysed (modified intention-to-treat).\[6\]
- Patients were 67.4% female and 88.5% White, with a mean age of 33.9 and an eligible age range of 18–70.\[6\]
- Outcomes were tracked for up to 16 weeks. Patients attended a mean of about 5.7 sessions.\[6\]

**Findings:**
- Matched patients improved faster on general impairment (d = 0.75), global distress on the SCL-10 (d = 0.50) and their most elevated problem domain (d = 0.60). No adverse events were reported.\[6\]
- On average, matched patients ended in the non-clinical range and usual-care patients stayed in the clinically impaired range.\[6\]
- Better matches did better: d = 1.25 when the therapist was effective on the patient's top 3 domains, 1.00 for the single top domain, and 0.75 for "not ineffective anywhere."\[6\]
- Under usual assignment, **36.1% of patients went to a therapist with no match and at least one ineffective domain.**\[6\] Avoiding weak areas is as important as finding strengths.

**Limitations:**
- Short follow-up of 16 weeks or less, with no long-term data.
- Mostly White adults in one US system. No adolescents.
- Many eligible people declined: 870 would not view the consent form, and most simply wanted an appointment fast.\[6\]
- About 24% of randomised patients were not analysed.
- Most "matches" were low level: 58.6% of matched patients got only level 5.\[6\]
- Every therapist needed a large stock of outcome data before the trial.
- The TOP developer (Kraus) is a co-author and runs the company behind the tool (Outcome Referrals Inc.), which is a possible conflict of interest.\[6\]\[7\]
- No independent replication has been found yet.

**Why it matters for you:** This is the only RCT showing that *matching on measured therapist strengths* causes better outcomes. It gives you a ready-made algorithm design: domain profiles, a "do no harm" exclusion rule and a ranked shortlist.

### 3. Follow-up studies from the same trial
- **Boswell JF, Constantino MJ, Coyne AE, Kraus DR. (2022).** For whom does a match matter most? Patient-level moderators of evidence-based patient-therapist matching. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 90(1), 61–74. doi:10.1037/ccp0000644. Matching helped **more for patients with higher baseline severity and more elevated problem domains (complexity)**, and more for racial/ethnic minority patients.\[8\] *Why it matters:* if you can only match carefully for some patients, put severe and complex cases first.
- **Coyne AE, Constantino MJ, Boswell JF, Gaines AN, Kraus DR. (2024).** Therapist-level moderators of patient-therapist match effectiveness in community psychotherapy. *Adm Policy Ment Health*, 51(5), 738–752. doi:10.1007/s10488-024-01360-8. The study had 206 patients and 40 therapists. Matching mattered most for therapists whose effectiveness varied widely across domains ("spread"), and for therapists whose caseloads were more severe or had more substance-misuse or violence cases.\[9\]\[10\]
- **Constantino MJ, Boswell JF, Coyne AE, Muir HJ, Gaines AN, Kraus DR. (2023).** Therapist perceptions of their own measurement-based, problem-specific effectiveness. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 91(8), 474–484. doi:10.1037/ccp0000813. The study had 50 therapists and 1,363 past patients. "For all but one outcome domain, therapists were no better than chance at predicting their measurement-based effectiveness classification." Therapists who overestimated their strengths had patients with worse outcomes.\[11\]\[12\]\[13\] *Why it matters:* **self-reported specialties are a weak basis for clinical matching**, which is the main argument for collecting outcome data. (I read this from the abstract only, not the full text.)
- **Walfish S, McAlister B, O'Donnell P, Lambert MJ. (2012).** An investigation of self-assessment bias in mental health providers. *Psychological Reports*, 110(2), 639–644. doi:10.2466/02.07.17.PR0.110.2.639-644. In a survey of 129 clinicians, the average self-rating was the 80th percentile, nobody rated themselves below the 50th, and 25% put themselves at the 90th or above.\[14\] This is the same overconfidence problem, at the level of overall skill.

### 4. Are therapists differentially effective by problem type?
- **Kraus DR, Castonguay L, Boswell JF, Nordberg SS, Hayes JA. (2011).** Therapist effectiveness: Implications for accountability and patient care. *Psychotherapy Research*, 21(3), 267–276. doi:10.1080/10503307.2011.563249. The study covered 6,960 patients and 696 therapists. "Effective" therapists had d = 1.00–1.52 in a domain and "harmful" ones had d = −0.91 to −1.49. A therapist's effectiveness in one domain correlated poorly with other domains, and\[15\] **no therapist was reliably effective in all domains**.\[16\]\[17\]\[18\] This is the basis for domain-specific matching.\[19\]
- **Kraus DR, Bentley JH, Alexander PC, Boswell JF, Constantino MJ, Baxter EE, Castonguay LG. (2016).** Predicting therapist effectiveness from their own practice-based evidence. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 84(6), 473–483. doi:10.1037/ccp0000083. The study had 59 therapists and 3,540 clients, with risk adjustment by random forests. Each therapist's first 30 clients were used to classify them, and that classification predicted later performance: in the authors' words, "Therapists classified as 'exceptional' were significantly more likely to remain above average with future cases." Effectiveness was "relatively stable, although somewhat domain specific." How much outcome the therapist explained differed by domain: about 18% for substance misuse and quality of life, but only about 2–4% for mania and psychosis.\[7\]\[19\]
- **Conflicting evidence — Nissen-Lie HA, Goldberg SB, Hoyt WT, et al. (2016).** Are therapists uniformly effective across patient outcome domains? *J Counseling Psychology*, 63(4), 367–378. doi:10.1037/cou0000151. The study used 5,828 US college counselling patients and 616 Swedish patients, measured on the OQ-45 and CORE-OM. "Therapists effective (or ineffective) within one outcome domain are also effective within another."\[20\] On this evidence, effectiveness looks mostly *global*.\[20\]
- **How to read the conflict:** the two camps used different tools and methods.\[21\] TOP covers very different problem areas, such as substance use vs depression, while the OQ-45 and CORE-OM subscales overlap more. A sensible reading is that therapists differ mainly in overall effectiveness, with real but smaller problem-specific differences that show up when domains are clearly separate.\[7\] **Your algorithm should score both overall effectiveness and domain-specific effectiveness.**

### 5. How much outcome is due to the therapist?
- **Johns RG, Barkham M, Kellett S, Saxon D. (2019).** A systematic review of therapist effects: A critical narrative update and refinement to Baldwin and Imel's (2013) review. *Clinical Psychology Review*, 67, 78–93. doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2018.08.004. The review covered 20 studies from 2012–2016 (3,743 therapists and 123,039 patients), and 19 of them found therapist effects. The authors concluded that "approximately 5% of the variance in outcomes was attributable to the therapist," which sits "within the 3–7% indicated by the previous systematic review (Baldwin & Imel 2013)." Effects were smaller in trials (about 3%) and larger in routine care and for more severe patients. The range across studies was wide.\[22\]
- **Baldwin SA, Imel ZE. (2013).** Therapist effects: Findings and methods. In Lambert MJ (Ed.), *Bergin and Garfield's Handbook of Psychotherapy and Behavior Change* (6th ed., pp. 258–297). Wiley. This meta-analysis found roughly **3% in RCTs and 7% in naturalistic settings** (often summarised as 3–7%), with individual studies ranging from 0% to about 55%.\[23\]\[24\]\[25\]
- **Crits-Christoph P, et al. (1991).** Meta-analysis of therapist effects in psychotherapy outcome studies. *Psychotherapy Research*, 1(2), 81–91. This is the foundational study: an average of 8.6% across 27 treatment groups, with less variability when treatment manuals and experienced therapists were used.\[26\]\[27\]
- **Saxon D, Barkham M. (2012).** Patterns of therapist variability: Therapist effects and the contribution of patient severity and risk. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 80(4), 535–546. doi:10.1037/a0028898. In 10,786 NHS primary-care patients treated by 119 therapists (2000–2008), the authors found a "therapist effect of 6.6% for average patient severity, but it ranged from 1% to 10% as patient non-risk scores increased." Recovery rates for individual therapists ranged from 23.5% to 95.6%.
- **What it means:** 5% sounds small, but across a caseload it separates therapists whose patients recover at very different rates. The therapist matters most for severe patients,\[28\] so **severity should weight how hard the system works to find a strong match.**\[29\]

### 6. Matching treatment intensity to severity (stratified vs stepped care)
- **Delgadillo J, Ali S, Fleck K, Agnew C, Southgate A, Parkhouse L, Cohen ZD, DeRubeis RJ, Barkham M. (2022).** Stratified care vs stepped care for depression: A cluster randomized clinical trial. *JAMA Psychiatry*, 79(2), 101–108. doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2021.3539.\[30\]
  - Design: 951 adults at 4 English IAPT (now NHS Talking Therapies) services, with 30 clinicians randomised. In the stratified arm, a machine-learning model sorted patients at assessment into "standard" cases (sent to low-intensity guided self-help) or "complex" cases (sent straight to high-intensity therapy).\[30\]
  - Model inputs: PHQ-9, GAD-7, functional impairment, personality traits, employment and ethnicity.\[30\]
  - Result: reliable and clinically significant depression improvement was **52.3% vs 45.1%** (OR 1.40). Stratified care cost £104.5 more per patient because more patients went to high-intensity care (56.9% vs 29.1%), and it was judged cost-effective.\[30\]
  - *Why it matters:* this is RCT evidence that **routing by predicted severity and complexity at intake beats "fail-first" stepped care**, using the kind of questionnaire data your website could collect.
- **Delgadillo J, Rubel J, Barkham M. (2020).** Towards personalized allocation of patients to therapists. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 88(9), 799–808. doi:10.1037/ccp0000507. The study used 4,849 IAPT CBT patients and 68 therapists. Machine learning found **17 classes of patient-to-therapist matches** in which some therapists did better with specific patient subgroups, and these were tested on the next year's data.\[31\]\[32\] This is proof-of-concept only, with no prospective trial.\[33\]

### 7. Personalised treatment selection (precision mental health)
- **DeRubeis RJ, Cohen ZD, Forand NR, Fournier JC, Gelfand LA, Lorenzo-Luaces L. (2014).** The Personalized Advantage Index: Translating research on prediction into individualized treatment recommendations. A demonstration. *PLoS ONE*, 9(1), e83875. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0083875. The study combined five baseline variables to predict whether each depressed patient would do better with cognitive therapy or antidepressants.\[34\]\[35\] *Why it matters:* it is the template for "which treatment for which person." It was retrospective, though, and predicted advantages are often small.
- **Huibers MJH, Cohen ZD, Lemmens LHJM, et al. (2015).** Predicting optimal outcomes in cognitive therapy or interpersonal psychotherapy for depressed individuals using the Personalized Advantage Index approach. *PLoS ONE*, 10(11), e0140771. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0140771. This applied the same method to CT vs IPT. For about 17% of patients, the predicted advantage was negligible.\[36\]\[37\]
- **Lutz W, Deisenhofer AK, Rubel J, Bennemann B, Giesemann J, Poster K, Schwartz B. (2022).** Prospective evaluation of a clinical decision support system in psychological therapy. *J Consult Clin Psychol*, 90(1), 90–106. doi:10.1037/ccp0000642. This tested the Trier Treatment Navigator in an RCT with 538 patient–therapist pairs (335 with the system, 203 without). Patients did better by **about d = 0.3 when therapists followed the recommended strategy** in the first 10 sessions.\[38\]\[39\] The effect depended on therapists finding the feedback useful.\[40\] *Why it matters:* decision support works best as advice to clinicians, not as an automatic decision.\[41\]

### 8. Modality fit for young people
- **Weisz JR, Kuppens S, Ng MY, et al. (2017).** What five decades of research tells us about the effects of youth psychological therapy. *American Psychologist*, 72(2), 79–117. doi:10.1037/a0040360. The meta-analysis covered 447 studies and 30,431 youths. The average effect was **0.46**,\[42\]\[43\] but it varied a lot by problem: **anxiety 0.61, depression 0.29, multiple problems 0.15 (not significant).** One moderator analysis also found smaller effects in adolescents than in children.\[44\]\[45\] *Why it matters:* modality and problem fit are real for young people. CBT-type treatment for anxiety is well supported, effects for youth depression are smaller, and complex mixed presentations are the weakest area. That supports routing complex young people to more intensive, multidisciplinary care.

### 9. Australian and youth-specific evidence
- **IAR-DST (Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing).** This is the national tool PHNs and GPs use to recommend one of **5 levels of care** in Australia's stepped care model, based on **8 assessment domains**. There are separate rating guides for adolescents (12–17) and adults (18–64), and the 2021–22 Budget allocated $34.2 million to roll it out. It does *not* judge urgency; acute risk overrides it.\[46\]\[47\]\[48\]\[49\] *Why it matters:* it is the locally accepted way to match severity to level of care, so your system should match its levels rather than invent its own.
- **IAR-DST in young adults (PubMed ID 42289850, 2026).** This study rated 104 young people aged 18–24 at an Adelaide tertiary community mental health service, and 63% were rated as needing high-intensity care. Higher recommended levels lined up with greater service use. It was a small retrospective file review, and more validation is needed.\[50\]
- **Hickie IB, Scott EM, Cross SP, et al. (2019).** Right care, first time: a highly personalised and measurement-based care model to manage youth mental health. *Med J Aust*, 211(S9), S3–S46. doi:10.5694/mja2.50383. This Sydney (Brain and Mind Centre) model argues for **staged care**: multidimensional assessment plus clinical staging decide intensity from the start, instead of "fail-first" stepped care. Related work: Iorfino F, et al. (2021), *Front Public Health*, doi:10.3389/fpubh.2021.621862, an automated clinical staging protocol; and Capon W, et al. (2022), *BMC Medicine*, doi:10.1186/s12916-022-02666-w, showing that clinical stage predicts different clinical and functional outcomes in young people.\[51\]\[52\]\[53\]
- **headspace outcomes (Rickwood, McEachran, Saw, Telford, Trethowan & McGorry, PLOS ONE, 2023, 18(6): e0282040; doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0282040).** The study covered 58,233 young people aged 12–25 at 108 centres. 75.2% presented with depression or anxiety, and CBT was the most common treatment. The authors report that "over one-third had significant improvements in psychological distress and a similar proportion in psychosocial functioning; just under half improved in self-reported quality of life," and that significant improvement on any of the three outcomes "was shown for 70.96%."
- **Hickie IB, Rosenberg S, et al. (2025),** *Aust N Z J Psychiatry*, "Novel youth mental health services in Australia" (doi:10.1177/00048674241297542). This paper reports "less than a third achieving significant improvement," with reliable change of "24.5% for psychological distress (self-reported K10), 29.9% for functioning (clinician-rated SOFAS)." It also argues that headspace applies clinical staging without established training or inter-rater reliability. *Note the disagreement:* the two papers reach different conclusions from overlapping data, and the gap is about which outcomes count.
- **Better Access evaluation (Pirkis et al., University of Melbourne, 2022, for the Department of Health).** The Australian Government's August 2024 response to the evaluation states that "approximately 1.3 million people receive psychological therapy through Better Access each year." Outcomes were best for people who started with more severe symptoms. A published critique (PMC10251454) highlights deterioration in 20–40% of patients depending on the method used, so watching for deterioration should be built in.
- **Gap:** I found **no Australian or youth study that tests matching young people to specific therapists based on outcomes.** Your project would be extending adult evidence, and you should say so plainly in your capstone.

## Recommendations: Building the Matching Algorithm

**Stage 1 — Safety and level of care (do this first):**
- Screen for acute risk (suicidality, self-harm, psychosis, safety). Positive screens skip matching and go to crisis pathways such as 000, Lifeline or the local mental health line. Matching should never delay urgent care.\[48\]
- Map the remaining patients to IAR-DST levels 1–5 using its 8 domains. Use the adolescent rating guide for ages 16–17 and the adult guide for ages 18–24.\[46\]\[47\]

**Stage 2 — Patient clinical profile (questionnaires):**
- **Distress/severity:** K10, the standard distress measure in Australian primary mental health care.
- **Problem-specific scales:** PHQ-9 (depression) and GAD-7 (anxiety), plus short screens for substance use, eating, sleep and trauma.
- **Functioning:** e.g., Work and Social Adjustment Scale, or clinician-rated SOFAS later on.
- **Problem-domain profile:** rank the patient's top 3 elevated domains, as in the Constantino trial.
- **Complexity:** number of elevated domains, comorbidity and past treatment. These predicted larger match benefits and justified high-intensity routing in Delgadillo (2022).
- **Keep it short:** PHQ-9 + GAD-7 + K10 is 26 items. Long intake forms reduce sign-up, as the 870 decliners in the Constantino trial showed.\[6\]

**Stage 3 — Therapist profile:**
- **Hard filters:** registration type (psychologist, clinical psychologist, mental health social worker or OT), Medicare/Better Access eligibility, the age groups they see (16–17 vs adults), availability, telehealth and cost/bulk billing.
- **Training/modality:** formal training in evidence-based treatments for each problem (e.g., CBT/exposure for anxiety, trauma-focused CBT/EMDR for PTSD, family-based treatment for eating disorders, DBT for self-harm/emotion dysregulation). Use these as the *cold-start* proxy.
- **Self-reported specialties:** collect them, but weight them low. Therapists judge their own strengths no better than chance (Constantino et al., 2023).\[1\]
- **Outcome track record (the long-term goal):** session-by-session PHQ-9/GAD-7/K10 from consenting patients, risk-adjusted for baseline severity. Classify each therapist per domain as effective, neutral or ineffective only after enough cases. The trial required 15 or more historical cases; Kraus (2016) used 30.\[6\]\[18\]

**Stage 4 — Ranking rule (a simple, defensible version):**
1. Exclude therapists measured as *ineffective* on any of the patient's elevated domains ("do no harm").
2. Rank by: effective on the top 3 domains > effective on the top domain > neutral. Before outcome data exists, use trained-in evidence-based modality as the fallback.
3. Give more weight to fit for high-severity or complex patients (Boswell 2022; Saxon & Barkham 2012).
4. Show the patient a **shortlist of 2–3 therapists**, as the trial did, and let preferences such as gender, cultural background and format break ties. Preference accommodation reduces dropout (Swift 2018).\[54\]
5. Track outcomes and flag deterioration, which feeds back into therapist profiles over time.

**Self-management actions:** tie these to severity level. Guided self-help and digital CBT suit lower IAR-DST levels, as the low-intensity arm in Delgadillo (2022) did. Present them as *supplements* while waiting, never as replacements for higher-level care.

## Caveats
- **Cold start:** new systems and new therapists have no outcome data, and real Medicare providers will not hand student projects their outcomes. Assumption: your capstone should use simulated or synthetic outcome data and say so. A sensible approach is to start every therapist at "neutral" and update as cases accumulate; Bayesian shrinkage stops a few lucky cases from swinging the rating.
- **Small numbers are noisy:** with fewer than about 15–30 cases per therapist per domain, ratings are unreliable. Many problem domains will rarely reach that threshold.
- **Evidence transfer:** the key matching RCT used US adults (88.5% White) and the stratified care RCT used English adults. Neither tested 16–24-year-olds or Australia's Medicare/GP-referral pathway. Under Better Access, patients still need a GP Mental Health Treatment Plan. People under 18 raise consent and privacy issues that this report has not researched.
- **Contested science:** whether therapists have real problem-specific strengths is disputed (Kraus vs Nissen-Lie). Measures like TOP also carry a commercial interest.
- **Measurement burden and gaming:** too many questionnaires cut uptake. Publishing therapist ratings can push therapists to avoid hard cases, so you need risk adjustment and should not rank therapists publicly.
- **Ethics/regulation:** ranking clinicians and handling health data of minors raises Australian Privacy Act and AHPRA advertising issues. Get expert advice before any real-world use.

## Summary: Strongest Evidence-Based Clinical-Fit Criteria
1. **Risk first:** acute risk overrides matching.
2. **Severity/complexity → level of care** (IAR-DST levels; RCT support from Delgadillo et al., 2022).
3. **Avoid therapists with measured poor outcomes in the patient's problem areas** (36% of usual-care patients landed with one in Constantino et al., 2021).
4. **Prefer therapists with measured effectiveness on the patient's top 1–3 problem domains** (d = 0.75–1.25 in Constantino et al., 2021). This matters most for severe and complex patients (Boswell et al., 2022).
5. **Until outcome data exists, use training in an evidence-based modality for the specific problem** (e.g., CBT for youth anxiety; Weisz et al., 2017), not therapists' self-rated specialties (Constantino et al., 2023).
6. **Use preference as the final layer** on a clinically filtered shortlist (Swift et al., 2018), and keep collecting outcomes so the system learns over time.
